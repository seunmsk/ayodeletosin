The Blank Slate Advantage: Can Africa Leapfrog to a More Sustainable Built Environment?

We do not always have to catch up. In some areas, we can build differently from the start.

TABy Tosin Ayodele · 7 min read

When I first wrote about the “Blank Slate Advantage” in September 2025, I was thinking about what looks, at first, like one of Africa's biggest disadvantages: the scale of infrastructure we still need to build.

Homes, offices, hospitals, schools, retail spaces, transport infrastructure, entire neighbourhoods and cities will have to expand to accommodate a rapidly urbanising population.

The scale of that challenge is enormous. Africa's urban population is projected to grow from about 700 million to 1.4 billion by 2050, meaning that the continent's cities will need to accommodate roughly 700 million additional residents over the next three decades. citiesalliance.org, issuu.com

But I have started to see another side to this story.

Infrastructure that has not yet been built also represents decisions that have not yet been made.

What materials will we use? How will buildings be cooled? Where will their energy come from? How much water will they consume? How will they cope with heat, flooding or unreliable utilities? And how expensive will they be to operate twenty or thirty years after construction?

That is what I mean by the Blank Slate Advantage.

Not that Africa is literally a blank slate. It isn't. Our cities already contain ageing buildings, infrastructure deficits and systems that urgently need investment. Many existing assets will have to be repaired, upgraded and retrofitted.

My argument is slightly different.

Where we need to build anyway, we have an opportunity to make some of these decisions differently from the beginning.

Learning from the cost of fixing things later

Working in facilities management has made this idea much more concrete for me.

Facilities teams frequently receive buildings after the important choices have already been made. The orientation is fixed. Cooling systems have been installed. Materials selected. Lighting systems specified. Water infrastructure designed.

We can improve how those systems operate, and sometimes the results can be significant.

At Benin City Mall, for example, an assessment I led identified avoidable energy waste associated with cooling and lighting. Changes to operating practices and lighting subsequently contributed to a 27% reduction in energy consumption.

That experience showed me the value of improving existing buildings.

But it also left me wondering:

What if some of the inefficiencies facilities teams spend years managing could have been avoided at the design and development stage?

That question has gradually changed how I think about Africa's infrastructure challenge.

Leapfrogging should be practical, not rhetorical

“Leapfrogging” is an attractive word.

It can also become meaningless if we don't explain what it looks like in practice.

For the built environment, I think it begins with relatively straightforward questions.

Can passive design reduce a building's dependence on mechanical cooling?

Can water efficiency be considered before plumbing systems and fixtures are specified?

Can drainage and flood resilience be designed around the climate conditions a development is likely to face over its lifetime?

Can building-management systems measure energy and water consumption from the beginning rather than being added years later?

Can developers assess the long-term operating consequences of material and equipment choices, rather than focusing mainly on initial cost?

None of these ideas is revolutionary on its own.

The opportunity lies in making them normal.

Africa's solar opportunity illustrates the point

Energy is perhaps the clearest example.

Africa has around 60% of the world's best solar resources but has historically accounted for only a very small share of installed global solar photovoltaic capacity. mg.co.za, knowledge.…gyinst.org

That gap is frustrating, but it also illustrates the scale of the opportunity.

For a new facility, the question does not necessarily need to be:

How do we add renewable energy later?

It can be:

How should energy be considered from the beginning?

Solar will not be the right answer for every building or every location. Neither should renewable energy distract from reducing demand in the first place.

A building that wastes energy does not become efficient simply because solar panels are installed on the roof.

The sequence matters.

First, understand demand.

Reduce avoidable consumption.

Design for efficiency.

Then determine how renewable energy fits the operating requirements and economics of the asset.

That is a more useful definition of leapfrogging than simply installing newer technology.

Born with data, not made smart later

The same principle applies to building technology.

When I originally wrote about this idea, I used the phrase “born digital, not retrofitted.” I still believe in the principle, although I now think about it less in terms of technology for technology's sake.

A genuinely smart building should help people make better decisions.

That could mean installing energy and water metering that shows where resources are being consumed. It could as well mean using building-management systems to adjust equipment schedules to actual demand or identifying faults early enough to prevent larger failures.

Over time, reliable building-performance data also gives owners something they often lack: a baseline.

Without knowing how a building performs today, it becomes difficult to prove that an intervention improved it tomorrow.

Digital systems therefore matter not because artificial intelligence, sensors or smart buildings sound futuristic, but because better information can lead to better decisions.

Resilience cannot be added as an afterthought either

Sustainability also means preparing buildings for the conditions in which they will actually operate.

Across African cities, those conditions can include extreme heat, heavy rainfall, flooding, water constraints and unreliable energy supply.

A building can be beautiful and still perform poorly when those realities are ignored.

Resilience therefore needs to move closer to the beginning of development decisions.

How will water leave the site during intense rainfall?

How will indoor temperatures be managed during extreme heat?

What happens if grid electricity becomes unavailable?

Can the building continue functioning during disruption?

These are design and investment questions before they become facilities-management problems.

But someone still has to pay for it

This is where my thinking has changed most since I first wrote about the Blank Slate Advantage.

It is easy to say Africa should build sustainably.

It is harder to answer the next question:

How do we persuade the people financing and developing these assets to make different choices?

Good intentions are not a business case.

Developers, investors and property owners have to consider capital costs, operating expenditure, financing, occupancy, risk and returns.

Sustainability therefore has a much better chance of being adopted when its environmental benefits can be considered alongside its commercial implications.

That does not mean every climate or environmental decision needs a short payback period.

Some benefits, particularly resilience and avoided future risk, are difficult to reduce to a simple financial calculation.

But where we can demonstrate that reducing energy or water consumption also lowers operating costs, improves building performance or avoids future corrective expenditure, we should.

The International Finance Corporation's EDGE programme reflects this connection between resource efficiency and commercial outcomes. Its work in Nigeria has focused on reducing energy and water consumption and the energy embodied in construction materials while also reducing utility and operating costs. disclosures.ifc.org, disclosures.ifc.org

That combination matters.

Sustainability becomes much easier to scale when it makes sense to the people responsible for approving and financing it.

The people living with our decisions

There is another reason why getting these choices right matters.

The people who experience a building do not necessarily choose how it was designed. An office worker does not select the cooling system. A tenant does not determine the building's orientation.

A family buying or renting a home may have had no influence over its water efficiency, insulation or materials. Communities living around developments may not have determined how drainage, waste or environmental risks were considered.

Yet people ultimately live with those decisions, sometimes for decades.

That is where sustainability becomes a question of climate justice as well as engineering and finance.

The consequences of poor development decisions are not always borne by the people who made them.

If Africa is going to build at the scale its urban growth requires, we should care not only about how much infrastructure we create, but also about who inherits the consequences of the choices we make today.

So, can Africa leapfrog?

I believe we can in some areas.

But I think the answer requires more humility than my original statement implied.

Africa cannot simply skip every stage of development. We face difficult constraints around financing, skills, regulation, infrastructure, technology and institutional capacity. Some existing assets will need expensive retrofits regardless of what we do next.

And sustainability itself has trade-offs.

The newest technology is not automatically the most appropriate. A green certification does not guarantee that a building will perform well in practice. Solar panels cannot compensate for fundamentally wasteful design. Imported sustainability solutions will not always fit local climatic, economic or social realities.

Leapfrogging will therefore require more than adopting what other markets have already developed.

It will require learning from them, adapting what works and being willing to design solutions around African conditions.

That is the opportunity I see.

Africa's infrastructure deficit is real. But many of the buildings and systems we will rely on in 2050 have not yet been built.

That means many important decisions have not yet been locked in either.

We may not have a completely blank slate.

But we still have space to choose differently.

The question is what we decide to build on it.
