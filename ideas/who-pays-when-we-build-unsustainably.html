Who Pays When We Build Unsustainably?

The people who live with development decisions are not always the people who make them

TABy Tosin Ayodele · 5 min read

A building begins with decisions.

Someone decides where it will sit, how it will be oriented, what materials will be used, how it will be cooled, how water will be managed and what level of resilience is worth paying for.

Eventually, those decisions become concrete, steel, glass, pipes and equipment.

Then somebody else inherits them.

A tenant inherits the electricity bill. A facilities team inherits the cooling system. A business inherits the disruption when drainage fails. A household inherits the cost of keeping an inefficient home comfortable.

That gap between who makes a decision and who lives with its consequences has become increasingly important to how I think about the built environment.

It also raises a question that I believe should sit closer to the centre of sustainability discussions:

When we build badly, who actually pays?

The obvious cost is not always the real cost

Construction decisions are often evaluated around the cost of delivering a project.

I understand why. Budgets matter.

But the amount required to construct a building is only one part of what that building will eventually cost.

Consider cooling.

A decision that appears economical during design may result in a building that depends heavily on mechanical air-conditioning. Once occupied, that decision becomes an electricity bill that arrives month after month.

The developer may no longer own the property. The consultant may have moved to another project.

But the building is still consuming energy, the occupant is still paying, and facilities team is still trying to make the system work more efficiently.

This is one of the reasons my experience in facilities management has changed the way I look at development decisions. Facilities teams often meet a building at the point where earlier choices become operating realities.

And by then, some choices are expensive to change.

Think about water

The same principle applies to water.

If water efficiency is considered early, developers can make choices around fixtures, metering, rainwater harvesting or reuse systems while designing the building.

But if ignored, the implications do not disappear.

They simply show up later as consumption, cost or pressure on supply.

Again, the person dealing with the consequence may not be the person who made the original decision.

Then there is resilience

This becomes even more serious when we think about climate risk.

Imagine a commercial property in an area exposed to increasingly intense rainfall.

Drainage may look like one line in a development budget. But if the provision is inadequate, the hidden cost could eventually include flooding, damaged equipment, interrupted businesses, repairs, lost income and rising insurance costs.

And the impact does not necessarily stop at the property boundary.

Poorly considered development can affect surrounding drainage, traffic, access and communities.

The cheapest decision today can become someone else's expensive problem tomorrow.

That does not mean every project needs the most sophisticated or expensive solution available.

It means cost needs a longer memory.

This is where climate justice becomes practical

For a long time, I associated climate justice primarily with large global questions: which countries produced the most emissions, which countries are most vulnerable, and who should finance the transition.

Those questions are important.

But I increasingly see a much more local version of climate justice in the built environment.

A person does not have to participate in a development decision to experience its consequences.

An office worker may have no influence over whether their workplace has adequate cooling or natural ventilation.

A tenant may have had no say in how energy-efficient a building is but still pays service charges linked to its performance.

A family may have had no role in designing the drainage around their neighbourhood but lives with the consequences when heavy rainfall comes.

The relationship is not always simple, and not every environmental problem can be traced to one decision-maker.

Still, the principle matters.

The ability to make a decision and the ability to escape its consequences are not always held by the same people.

That, to me, is one of the practical questions climate justice asks of the built environment.

Better buildings need broader questions

When assessing a development choice, perhaps we should ask more than:

What will this cost to build?

We could also ask:

What will it cost to operate?

What resources will it consume?

How will it perform during extreme weather?

Who will pay when its systems are inefficient?

Who carries the risk if our assumptions turn out to be wrong?

How difficult will this decision be to reverse later?

These questions do not replace financial discipline. They improve it.

They encourage us to look at cost across the life of an asset rather than only at the point of construction.

And they bring people back into a sustainability conversation that can otherwise become dominated by certifications, technology and carbon calculations.

Sustainability should not become a luxury

There is another danger I think we need to acknowledge.

If sustainable buildings are treated primarily as premium products for organisations that can afford expensive technology and certification, we risk making the transition itself unequal.

Efficient lighting is useful because it uses less energy, not because it carries a sustainability label.

Passive cooling matters because it can reduce dependence on mechanical systems.

Water efficiency matters because water is a resource, whether the building has a green certification or not.

Good drainage matters because people need buildings and communities that can continue functioning when it rains heavily.

The goal should not simply be to build impressive “green buildings.”

It should be to make better building performance increasingly normal.

Who pays?

This is the question I keep coming back to.

When we choose an inefficient cooling system, who pays?

When water is wasted, who pays?

When drainage is inadequate, who pays?

When a building needs an expensive retrofit ten years after completion, who pays?

Sometimes it is the owner.

Sometimes it is the tenant.

Sometimes it is a business.

Sometimes the cost is absorbed by a community or a public institution.

Sometimes the environmental cost is harder to see.

But somebody usually inherits the decision.

That is why I think sustainability needs to enter the conversation earlier.

Not because every building can be perfect nor because every environmental consideration outweighs every financial one.

But because the choices we make about buildings can remain long after the people who made them have moved on.

If we think more carefully about who eventually pays, we may make better decisions about what, how and for whom we build.
